What is a cyber risk assessment?

A cyber risk assessment finds what could go wrong with your systems and data, how likely it is, and how much harm it would do. It tells you which problems to fix first, and which ones you can live with.

In short

Rank your risks, then fix the ones that matter most

Every business has more security work than time or money. A risk assessment puts that work in order. It looks at your people, your processes and your technology, and gives each risk two ratings: how likely it is to happen, and how bad it would be if it did.

It isn't a one-off project. Your business, your suppliers and the attacks you face all change, so the assessment is the start of a habit: assess, fix, check again.

Gap or risk?

Two questions that belong together

A gap assessment checks your controls against a standard, such as Cyber Essentials or ISO 27001. It answers: what's missing?

A risk assessment looks at what could go wrong, how likely it is and how bad it would be. It answers: what matters most?

On its own, a gap list can feel endless, and a risk list can float free of any standard your customers recognise. Our gap assessment joins the two: every gap we find becomes a draft risk in your risk register, rated for likelihood and impact, and we agree the ratings with you.

What it looks at

What you have, and what could go wrong

What you have. Your computers and phones, the software and cloud services you rely on, the data you hold, the people who use it, and the suppliers who can reach it. You can't protect what you haven't listed.

What could go wrong. The threats a firm like yours faces, such as a phishing email, ransomware, invoice fraud, a breach at a supplier or a lost laptop. Then the weak points they would use: missing security updates, sign-in without a second step, settings never changed from the default, or backups nobody has tried to restore.

How it works

Six steps

  1. 1Agree the scope

    Which parts of the business, which sites and which systems, and what you need the assessment to tell you.

  2. 2Choose the yardstick

    The standard to measure against, for example Cyber Essentials, ISO 27001, NIS2 or what your biggest customer asks for.

  3. 3List what you have

    Devices, software, cloud services, data, people and suppliers, and which of them the business can't run without.

  4. 4Find what could go wrong

    The threats you face, and the weak points in your set-up that they would use.

  5. 5Rate and record each risk

    Likelihood and impact, a named owner, and a decision: fix it, avoid it, insure it, or accept it.

  6. 6Fix, then check again

    Work through the fixes in order of risk, and re-assess on a regular cycle.

How risks are rated

Likelihood times impact

We rate likelihood and impact from 1 to 5 each, and multiply them. A score of 15 or more is high, 8 to 14 is medium, and below 8 is low. The scale matters less than using the same one every time, so this year's risks can be compared with last year's.

Each risk then gets an owner and a decision. Most are reduced with a fix. Some are avoided by stopping the activity, some are passed on through insurance or a contract, and some are accepted. Accepting a risk is a fair business choice, as long as a named director makes it and it's written down.

How often

Once a year, and after any big change

No standard sets one timetable for every firm. Our advice: a full assessment once a year, and again after any big change, such as a new system, a move to the cloud, buying another business or an incident. Between assessments, the board should see the top risks at least twice a year.

Who asks for it

Most standards start here

  • ISO 27001: assessing and treating information security risk is at the heart of the standardClauses 6.1 and 8.2
  • NIS2: risk analysis is the first of the security measures in-scope firms must takeArticle 21
  • DORA: EU financial firms must run an ICT risk management frameworkArticles 5 to 16
  • UK GDPR: security must be appropriate to the risk to people's dataArticle 32
  • SOC 2: risk assessment is one of the common criteriaCC3
  • PCI DSS: some requirements need a targeted risk analysis12.3.1
  • NCSC Cyber Assessment Framework and the Cyber Governance Code: both expect risk to be managed and overseenCAF A2

Cyber Essentials is the exception: it checks five technical controls and doesn't ask for a risk assessment. That's one reason it's a starting point, not the finish line.
